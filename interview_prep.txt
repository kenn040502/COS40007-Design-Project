--- Q&A ---

Q1: What is ARIMA and why did you choose it?
A: ARIMA stands for AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average. It is a classical
   time series model that captures patterns in past values (AR part), handles
   non-stationarity via differencing (I part), and corrects for residual errors
   (MA part). We chose it because Malaysia's CPI inflation is a univariate monthly
   time series with a long history going back to 1980, which is exactly what ARIMA
   is designed for. We did not have enough labelled future data to train a neural
   network, so a statistical model was the right choice here.

Q2: What is the ADF test and why did you do it?
A: ADF stands for Augmented Dickey-Fuller. It tests whether a time series is
   stationary — meaning its mean and variance do not drift over time. ARIMA
   requires stationary data to make reliable forecasts. If the test p-value is
   below 0.05 we treat the series as stationary (d=0); otherwise we apply one
   round of differencing (d=1) to remove the trend.
   Code: src/time_series.py lines 38-44 (check_stationarity function).
   The result sets the 'd' parameter in ARIMA(p,d,q).
   Code: src/time_series.py lines 53-54 inside select_order.

Q3: How did you pick the best ARIMA order (p, d, q)?
A: We ran a grid search over all combinations where p is 0 to 4 and q is 0 to 4.
   For each combination we fitted the model and recorded the BIC (Bayesian
   Information Criterion). BIC penalises model complexity more heavily than AIC,
   so it pushes us towards simpler, more generalisable models. We kept the (p,d,q)
   combination with the lowest BIC.
   Code: src/time_series.py lines 47-75 (select_order function).
   The outer loop is p (line 61), the inner loop is q (line 62), ARIMA is fitted
   at line 68, and the best BIC is tracked at lines 69-71.

Q4: Why did you skip p=0, q=0 in the grid search?
A: An ARIMA(0,d,0) model with no autoregressive or moving average terms would just
   produce the mean of the differenced series — it is not a meaningful time series
   model. We exclude it at line 63-64 in select_order so we do not waste a model
   slot on a degenerate case.

Q5: What metrics did you use to evaluate the forecast and why?
A: We used four metrics:
   - MAE (Mean Absolute Error): simple average of absolute errors.
   - RMSE (Root Mean Square Error): penalises large errors more heavily than MAE.
   - sMAPE (Symmetric MAPE): percentage error that is safe when the actual value
     is near zero. CPI inflation regularly crosses zero (deflation), so standard
     MAPE would blow up or be undefined. sMAPE is bounded between 0 and 200%.
     Code: src/time_series.py lines 92-104 (_smape function).
   - MASE (Mean Absolute Scaled Error): the model's MAE divided by the MAE of
     a naive random-walk forecast on the training data. MASE < 1 means our model
     beats just repeating the last observed value.
     Code: src/time_series.py lines 107-119 (_mase function).
   We deliberately excluded plain MAPE because inflation crosses zero.
   Code: evaluate_forecast at lines 122-137.

Q6: What is the naive baseline and why is it important?
A: The naive forecast simply carries the last observed value forward for all future
   steps (random walk). It is the simplest possible model and a fair lower bar:
   if our ARIMA cannot beat this, there is no point using it.
   Code: src/time_series.py lines 140-142 (naive_forecast function).
   MASE < 1 means ARIMA beats the naive baseline.

Q7: How did you test whether fuel prices help predict inflation?
A: We fitted a SARIMAX (ARIMAX) model using average fuel price as an exogenous
   variable, then compared its RMSE against a plain ARIMA on the same held-out
   24-month window. The percentage RMSE improvement tells us whether fuel adds
   real predictive signal beyond what ARIMA already captures on its own.
   We also extracted the fuel coefficient and its p-value to check statistical
   significance.
   Code: src/time_series.py lines 173-265 (compute_fuel_exog_model function).
   The honest caveat: the headline 2-year forecast stays univariate because we
   cannot know future fuel prices — fuel is useful for explanation, not
   operational forecasting.
   Code: lines 183-186 (the comment explaining this trade-off).

Q8: How did you clean the national inflation data?
A: The raw CSV from OpenDOSM (cpi_2d_inflation.csv) contained monthly records for
   14 spending divisions plus an 'overall' row going back to 1980. We:
   - Parsed the date column as datetime (line 42, preprocessing.py).
   - Converted inflation_yoy and inflation_mom to numeric, coercing errors to NaN
     (lines 45-46, preprocessing.py).
   - Mapped division codes like '01' to readable labels like 'Food & Non-Alcoholic
     Beverages' (line 47, preprocessing.py using DIVISION_LABELS dict lines 21-36).
   - Extracted only the 'overall' row for ARIMA, dropping rows where yoy is NaN
     because there is no prior-year reference before 1981 (build_overall_inflation,
     lines 93-100, preprocessing.py).

Q9: What does the 7,770 record figure represent?
A: The raw CPI inflation file has 14 division categories plus one overall row,
   each with monthly records from 1980 to 2026. That is roughly 555 months times
   14 divisions = ~7,770 total rows. For ARIMA we only used the 'overall' column,
   which is about 545 monthly observations after dropping the early NaN rows.

Q10: What would you do differently if you had more time?
A: I would explore SARIMA (seasonal ARIMA) to capture yearly patterns in
   inflation — for example, food prices typically spike before major holidays.
   I would also try a rolling-window evaluation instead of a single train/test
   split to get a more robust estimate of out-of-sample performance.

--- Q&A ---

Q1: What features did you engineer for each state and why those five?
A: We created five features per state:
   1. mean_cpi_index: average CPI index over all available months — captures the
      overall price level of the state.
   2. cpi_growth_rate: percentage change from the earliest to latest CPI index —
      captures how much prices have grown over time.
   3. cpi_volatility: standard deviation of monthly CPI — measures price stability;
      a high value means prices fluctuate a lot.
   4. income_mean_latest: most recent survey mean household income.
   5. income_median_latest: most recent survey median household income.
   We included both income measures because mean income is sensitive to high earners
   while median is more representative of the typical household.
   Code: src/preprocessing.py lines 103-140 (build_state_features function).
   Code: src/clustering.py lines 35-41 (FEATURE_COLS list).

Q2: Why did you standardise the features before clustering?
A: CPI index values are around 100-150, income values are in thousands of ringgit,
   and growth rates are percentages. Without standardisation, income would dominate
   the distance calculations simply because its numbers are larger, not because it
   is more important. StandardScaler transforms each feature to zero mean and unit
   variance so all five contribute equally.
   Code: src/clustering.py lines 45-49 (prepare_features function).

Q3: How did you choose the number of clusters (k)?
A: We ran a grid search over k from 2 to 8 (MAX_K = 8, which is roughly half the
   number of states — line 42 in clustering.py). For each k we computed four metrics:
   - Inertia (WCSS): total within-cluster sum of squares — lower is better but
     always decreases with more clusters, so we use the "elbow" shape.
   - Silhouette score: measures how well-separated clusters are, ranging from -1
     to +1; higher is better.
   - Davies-Bouldin index: average ratio of within-cluster scatter to between-cluster
     distance; lower is better.
   - Calinski-Harabasz score: ratio of between-cluster to within-cluster dispersion;
     higher is better.
   We selected k = argmax(silhouette) as our primary criterion.
   Code: src/clustering.py lines 52-72 (elbow_and_silhouette function).
   The final selection is at line 64: best_k = list(k_range)[np.argmax(silhouettes)].

Q4: Why did you use both K-Means and Hierarchical clustering?
A: K-Means requires you to specify k in advance, uses centroids, and assumes roughly
   spherical clusters. Hierarchical clustering (Ward linkage) builds a tree of
   merges without needing k upfront, making no shape assumptions. Using both lets
   us check whether the groupings are consistent across different algorithmic
   assumptions. If both methods produce similar cluster assignments, we have more
   confidence that the structure is real and not an artefact of the algorithm.

Q5: What is Ward linkage and why did you use it?
A: Ward linkage merges the two clusters whose union produces the smallest increase
   in total within-cluster variance. It tends to produce compact, equally-sized
   clusters and is generally the best-performing linkage method for socioeconomic
   data. We chose it over single or complete linkage because it minimises
   within-group heterogeneity.
   Code: src/clustering.py line 82 — AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=k, linkage="ward").

Q6: What is PCA and why did you use it?
A: Principal Component Analysis reduces high-dimensional data to fewer dimensions
   while retaining as much variance as possible. We have 5 features per state,
   which cannot be plotted directly. PCA projects them onto 2 axes (PC1, PC2) that
   capture the most variance, allowing us to draw a scatter plot where each state
   is a point and clusters can be visually inspected.
   Code: src/clustering.py lines 87-91 (pca_transform function).
   The axis labels in the plot show what percentage of variance each component
   explains: line 177 in clustering.py.

Q7: How did you label the clusters with meaningful names?
A: We ordered the clusters by their mean income in ascending order, then assigned
   the labels "Low Income & Low CPI", "Mid-Low Income & Mid-Low CPI",
   "Mid-High Income & High CPI", and "High Income & High CPI" from lowest to
   highest. This works because income and CPI price level are strongly correlated
   across Malaysian states — richer states tend to have higher price levels.
   Code: src/clustering.py lines 94-113 (assign_semantic_labels function).
   The ordering is at line 101: cluster_means["income_mean_latest"].argsort().

Q8: How did you validate that the clusters are genuine?
A: We used three validation scores after fitting:
   1. Silhouette score: if > 0.5, clusters are well-defined. We computed this for
      both K-Means and Hierarchical at lines 270 and 290 in clustering.py.
   2. Davies-Bouldin index: lower means clusters are more compact and separated.
   3. Calinski-Harabasz score: higher means denser, better-separated clusters.
   Consistent scores across both clustering methods confirm that the structure is
   not just an artefact of K-Means.

Q9: How did you clean the state CPI and income data?
A: For state CPI (cpi_2d_state.csv):
   - Parsed dates (line 55, preprocessing.py), converted index to numeric (line 58),
     dropped rows where index is NaN (line 60).
   State income (hh_income_state.csv):
   - Parsed dates (line 85), converted income_mean and income_median to numeric
     (lines 87-88), dropped rows where income_mean is NaN (line 89).
   Code: preprocessing.py lines 52-62 (load_cpi_state) and lines 82-90 (load_income_state).

Q10: Why is the maximum k set to 8?
A: We have 16 states. A general rule of thumb for clustering is that k should be
   at most roughly half the number of data points, otherwise each cluster ends up
   with very few members and the groupings lose interpretability. With k=8 we
   ensure each cluster has at least 2 states on average.
   Code: src/clustering.py line 42 — MAX_K = 8.

Q1: Why did you need to resample the fuel price data from weekly to monthly?
A: Our CPI inflation data is recorded monthly. To measure the correlation between
   fuel prices and inflation we need both series to share the same time frequency.
   We grouped the weekly fuel readings by month (using resample("MS") for month-start)
   and took the mean within each month. This preserves price variation across months
   while aligning with the CPI schedule.
   Code: src/preprocessing.py lines 65-79 (load_fuelprice function).
   The resample step is at line 77: df.set_index("date").resample("MS").mean().

Q2: What is Pearson correlation and what does it tell us here?
A: Pearson r measures the linear relationship between two variables, ranging from
   -1 (perfect negative) to +1 (perfect positive). In our context it tells us how
   closely fuel price movements track inflation movements. A positive r means when
   fuel goes up, inflation tends to go up too. We computed two variants:
   - Contemporaneous: fuel at month t vs inflation at month t.
   - Lag-1: fuel at month t vs inflation at month t+1 — tests if fuel LEADS inflation.
   Code: src/time_series.py lines 145-170 (compute_fuel_correlation function).
   Contemporaneous: line 160. Lag-1: line 161 (shifts the series by one).

Q3: Why did you measure lag-1 correlation specifically?
A: There is a plausible physical reason why fuel might lead inflation by a month:
   higher fuel prices increase transport and production costs, but businesses take
   time to pass those costs onto consumers. A one-month lag tests this mechanism.
   If the lag-1 correlation is stronger than the contemporaneous one, it suggests
   fuel price changes transmit into inflation with a delay.

A: It is a dual-panel chart in evaluation.py (plot_fuel_cpi_correlation, lines 49-95).
   Left panel: scatter plot of average fuel price vs overall inflation YoY, with
   an OLS regression line showing the overall trend and Pearson r on the label.
   Right panel: grouped bar chart showing contemporaneous and lag-1 correlations
   for each fuel type (RON95, RON97, Diesel, average).

Q1: How does the train/test split work for time series?
A: Unlike regular machine learning where you can shuffle data, time series must
   be split in order. We keep all observations except the last 24 months as
   training data, and the final 24 months are the test set. We never allow the
   model to see future data during training — this simulates real forecasting.
   Code: src/time_series.py lines 88-89 (train_test_split_ts function).
   train = series.iloc[:-24], test = series.iloc[-24:].

Q2: Why retrain on the full dataset for the final forecast?
A: The test split exists only to measure accuracy. Once we have confirmed the
   model performs well, we retrain on ALL available data (training + test) before
   generating the real forward forecast. Using more data gives the model a better
   picture of recent trends, which improves the 2-year ahead prediction.
   Code: src/time_series.py lines 568-572 in run_all — fit_arima on full series
   after evaluation, then saved to arima_fitted.pkl.

Q3: What are the 95% confidence intervals in the forecast plot?
A: The ARIMA model produces not just a single forecast value but also uncertainty
   bounds. The 95% confidence interval means that under the model's assumptions,
   the true future inflation value will fall inside the shaded band 95% of the time.
   The interval widens as we forecast further into the future because uncertainty
   accumulates. These come from the ARIMA's in-built get_forecast() method.
   Code: src/time_series.py lines 82-85 (_extract_forecast function) —
   fc.conf_int(alpha=0.05) returns the lower and upper bounds.
   They appear in the plot as the violet shaded region (line 407-412 in plot_forecast).

   268-381) yields progress dicts that the dashboard converts into SSE messages.

Q10: How did you handle caching to avoid re-running expensive steps?
A: ARIMA order selection (BIC grid search over 24 combinations) and model fitting
   are expensive. We cache the results in arima_results.json. On the next run, if
   the series length and order in the cache match the current data, we skip both
   the grid search and the expensive refits, loading the pre-fitted .pkl model
   instead. The cache is invalidated automatically if the data changes (different
   series length).
   Code: src/time_series.py lines 497-535 in run_all — the cache check logic.
   Similarly in run_live, lines 284-300.
   On the Streamlit side, @st.cache_data decorators on loaders prevent re-reading
   CSVs on every user interaction.

Q11: What is the matplotlib font cache and why did you commit it?
A: Matplotlib builds a font cache the first time it runs, scanning all system fonts.
   On cloud platforms (Streamlit Cloud, Render) this can take 2-3 minutes. We
   pre-built the cache locally and committed the outputs/mpl_cache/ directory to
   the repo. The pipeline sets MPLCONFIGDIR to point at it (run_pipeline.py line 23
   and streamlit_app.py line 25), so cloud deployments skip the expensive rebuild.

Q12: How did you organise large file storage?
A: Dataset CSVs from OpenDOSM are large (the state CPI file is several MB). We used
   Git LFS (Large File Storage) to track them, so they do not bloat the main git
   history. The outputs/ directory (figures, models) is also gitignored except for
   the mpl_cache, since outputs are regenerated by running the pipeline.

Q: What is the overall problem your project addresses?
A: Malaysia has been experiencing rising cost of living, particularly after the
   COVID-19 pandemic and fuel subsidy restructuring. Our system uses AI to:
   1. Forecast national CPI inflation 2 years ahead (ARIMA model).
   2. Cluster all 16 states by their economic profile (price level + income).
   3. Analyse whether fuel prices predict or drive inflation.
   The target audience is government policymakers who need evidence-based tools
   to understand and respond to cost-of-living pressures.

Q: Where did your data come from?
A: All four datasets were downloaded from OpenDOSM — Malaysia's official open
   data portal run by the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM). They are
   publicly available and regularly updated. We used:
   - cpi_2d_inflation.csv : national monthly CPI inflation by 14 categories (1980-2026)
   - cpi_2d_state.csv     : monthly CPI index by state (2010-present)
   - fuelprice.csv        : weekly retail fuel prices (2017-present)
   - hh_income_state.csv  : household income survey by state (1970-2022)
   Code: data/download_datasets.py — the download URLs and filenames.

Q: How does the pipeline ensure reproducibility?
A: Several mechanisms:
   1. All random seeds are fixed (random_state=42 in KMeans and PCA).
   2. The pipeline always runs the same 4 steps in the same order.
   3. Outputs (JSON, PKL, CSV) are versioned and cached — deleting them triggers
      a clean rerun from scratch.
   4. Working directory is set explicitly at the start of run_pipeline.py (line 17)
      so relative paths work regardless of how the script is invoked.

Q: Why did you use Streamlit for the dashboard?
A: Streamlit lets us write the entire dashboard in Python without needing
   JavaScript, HTML, or a separate frontend framework. It integrates directly with
   pandas DataFrames and Plotly charts, and deploys to Streamlit Cloud with a
   single push to GitHub. For a data science project with a small team this is far
   more efficient than building a custom React frontend.

Q: What is the test set size and why 24 months?
A: We held out the last 24 months (2 years) as the test set. This is long enough
   to capture seasonal patterns and economic cycles, while still leaving enough
   training data (40+ years for the national series) for the model to learn from.
   Two years also matches our forecast horizon, making the evaluation directly
   comparable to the real prediction task.

         Line 35 — FORECAST_HORIZON = 24.
